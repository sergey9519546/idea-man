# General innovation playbooks

Choose the playbook that matches the conductor's current diagnosis. These playbooks supply craft inside a chosen route; they do not supersede the user's scope, existing decisions, host instructions or capability limits. Named thinking methods are procedures for exploration, not proof of correctness or novelty.

## Frame an unclear or contested problem

Use when success, boundaries or stakeholder priorities are unclear. Recover the actual outcome, current state, affected people and non-negotiable constraints. Write competing framings only when they would lead to materially different work. Show what each framing excludes. Identify the missing decision or evidence that would settle the boundary.

Useful methods: reframing, assumption examination, stakeholder/system maps. Skip further framing when the task is already precise. A compelling problem statement is an output; it is not evidence that all stakeholders agree.

Example: "Increase engagement" might mean more useful completions, more frequent visits or longer time spent. Identify the intended benefit before optimizing a proxy that could make the experience worse.

## Calculate, estimate or model a quantity

Use when the requested result is numerical. Identify the quantity being asked for,
the inputs, definitions, units, assumptions and range in which the model applies.
Distinguish exact arithmetic, estimation, optimization and statistical inference;
an adoption prediction does not become a calculation merely because costs are known.
Derive missing values only when the supplied relationships justify them. Do not
silently substitute zero, a typical value or an invented measurement.

Choose a justified formula/model, calculate with an appropriate available tool when
useful for reliability, and check dimensions, bounds, material edge cases and a
separate sanity check proportional to consequence. Preserve direction and time basis
when combining rates. An unavailable calculator does not block simple arithmetic;
an uncheckable complex computation must retain its verification limit.

For coupled rates, parallel work, before/after comparisons or resource allocations,
write the quantity relationship before calculating. Use an available calculator or
code tool for the arithmetic and a dimensional/inverse check for the model; these
checks can stay internal when only the answer is requested. A previous assistant
answer is not a measured baseline. Recompute it from the supplied facts before
building further claims on it. Compare alternatives on the same time, unit and
resource basis, identifying exactly which assumption changed. Do not drop an existing
parallelism factor or count the same person/resource in simultaneous roles twice.

For a process with several stages, distinguish equipment throughput from the labor
needed to operate it. A shared scanner or machine does not imply an extra operator
or unattended operation. Check whether setup, processing and inspection compete for
the same people, and whether an initially empty pipeline delays the first output.
If that dependency is unspecified and changes the answer, give a clearly conditional
result or ask the one material question; do not headline an unconditional maximum.
For a claimed maximum, check both an upper bound and an attainable schedule under
the same assumptions. Apply this only when resource coupling is material; ordinary
independent-rate arithmetic still needs no intake or scheduling exercise.

Statistical descriptions and empirical inferences require different support. A
correct mean or percentage does not establish causation, significance or a population
effect. For an inference inspect the sample, denominator, comparison, measurement and
confounds using the relevant diagnosis/experiment guidance as well as numerical checks.
Do not invent sample sizes, uncertainty intervals or a fitted model's performance.

Output the requested quantity with appropriate precision, units, assumptions and
material uncertainty. A direct answer can be enough; do not add an invention round,
research project or new experiment when the requested calculation is already supported.

## Prove or inspect a formal claim

Use for a requested proof, derivation or counterexample. Establish definitions,
assumptions, domain and the exact claim. Check each necessary implication, boundary
case and quantifier; distinguish a sufficient example from a general argument.
An empirical pattern, plausible analogy or many passing examples is not a proof.
If the claim fails, provide a valid counterexample or identify the unsupported step.
Use an available formal checker only when appropriate and authorized; claim machine
verification only when actually performed. Report the argument and its limits at
the requested depth, without a compulsory creative-method exercise.

## Explain or coach at the requested level

Use when success means understanding or practicing rather than receiving a finished
decision/artifact. Recover the actual question, supplied level of understanding,
time/format constraints and whether the user wants an explanation, worked example,
question or feedback on an attempt. Do not invent demographic or ability assumptions.

Explain the key relationship accurately; use a concrete example or analogy only when
it helps, and state a consequential limit of the analogy. For factual or numerical
content retain the applicable source or calculation checks. An explanation need not
be an article, a brainstorm or a new research assignment.

In coaching, respect the requested pace and preserve the user's opportunity to reason.
If one question at a time is requested, provide that question or feedback on the actual
attempt without prematurely revealing the solution. Adapt after confusion; do not
repeat the same abstraction or force a quiz on someone who requested an answer.
The next requested learning interaction can complete the current turn. Do not claim
learning transfer or retention from a clear explanation or an unrun exercise.

When an attempt is supplied, inspect its first consequential incorrect or unsupported
step before moving on. Direct the next question or feedback at that step; repeating
the final target question does not respond to the attempt. Keep the requested pace
without giving away later steps. If the user switches to a solution-only answer,
stop coaching and check that solution against the original problem.

## Diagnose a failure, bottleneck or repeated disappointment

Use when an observed result needs explanation. Inspect the process, timing, flows, constraints and available measurements. Separate observations, causal hypotheses and possible remedies. Consider at least one plausible alternative explanation when causation is consequential. Look for disconfirming evidence and feedback loops rather than declaring the first correlation a root cause.

Useful methods: causal mapping, bottleneck analysis, counterfactuals, inversion and assumption tests. A repeated "why" question is useful only while its answers have evidence; it does not establish causality by repetition.

Output: best-supported explanation or unresolved alternatives, evidence limits, and the intervention/observation most likely to distinguish them. Do not add a wide invention tournament unless the user needs alternatives after diagnosis.

Example: a delivery queue might grow because demand increased, a downstream review blocks completion, or rework returns completed jobs. The same visible backlog does not justify the same remedy in all three cases.

## Invent alternatives under constraints

Use when the goal is sufficiently understood but the solution space is thin. Separate laws/real limits from habits and design conventions. Preserve actual hard constraints. Generate alternatives by changing mechanisms, relationships, timing, incentives, decomposition or resource use.

Useful methods: first principles, subtraction, contradiction resolution, analogical transfer, recombination and morphological exploration. Choose methods because of the diagnosed barrier. Do not automatically require subtraction, distant analogy or a fixed method count for every problem.

Recover the actual existing arrangement before proposing an improvement. Repeating
that arrangement is not a new mechanism. Check hard resource totals across the whole
proposal: reallocating a role must remove its former allocation or account for its
time, and an extra worker/tool cannot be assumed available. A mechanism that needs
a changed hard limit is outside the current feasible set; identify that condition
instead of presenting it as a compliant option. Label unmeasured effects and invented
scenario inputs as hypotheses, never as a typical or observed baseline.

For analogy, state the source mechanism, target mapping and where the correspondence may fail. For subtraction, test what function disappears and whether the remaining system still serves the user. For contradiction, specify the competing requirements and seek separation or redesign rather than hiding the tradeoff.

Output: requested number of distinct mechanisms, the strongest assumptions/tradeoffs, and a path to compare or test them. Novelty is a hypothesis unless the relevant landscape has been investigated; do not claim universal originality.

Example: five ways to monetize idle equipment could vary access, ownership, scheduling, bundling and risk allocation. Renaming the same rental offer five times does not satisfy the request.

## Controlled variations preserving explicit locks

Use when the user requests alternatives around an existing reference or chosen
direction. Inspect that exact source and recover the requested count, selected
candidate, explicit locks and permitted variation axes before generating variants.
The task can be exploration, refinement or a narrow property change; the word
"variation" does not authorize replacing the chosen mechanism.

Preserve fixed facts, offer, mechanism, identity, causality or other named properties.
Vary only the allowed dimensions. Tone, gesture, wording, sequence, proportion or
another permitted property can supply all the requested distinction. Mechanism
differences are useful only when that axis is allowed. Apply selected thinking
procedures within this boundary rather than treating a procedure as permission to
break a lock.

Deduplicate on the permitted axes. A shared locked mechanism is not a duplicate
during refinement. If the requested count cannot be made meaningfully distinct
without breaking a lock, explain the constraint instead of silently widening scope.
Check each variant against the reference, requested differences and protected
properties. Keep hypothetical effects separate from measured outcomes.

Output: the requested variants and their meaningful differences within the permitted
scope. For example, three tone variants of a selected offer preserve its offer,
facts and persuasion mechanism while changing only the authorized tone.

## Make a decision or choose a strategy

Use when viable options already exist. Define the decision, constraints, horizon, reversibility and criteria from the user's goals. Eliminate options that violate hard constraints. Compare remaining options using evidence appropriate to the decision; use ordinal judgments when numerical precision would be invented.

Useful methods: explicit tradeoffs, scenarios, sensitivity analysis, premortems and reversible experiments. Weighted matrices can reveal preferences but do not turn guessed inputs into measurements. Show which assumption would change the recommendation.

Output: recommendation/selection within delegated authority, decisive tradeoff, assumptions and a trigger for revisiting it. Do not restart brainstorming just because there are three options to compare.

Example: an automation investment can be attractive under high repetitive volume but fragile when exception handling dominates. Compare those scenarios before presenting a single confident ROI.

## Research a question or develop a hypothesis

Use when progress depends on what is true or what the available evidence supports. Distinguish supplied-material synthesis from current external verification. Trace claims to inspected sources; retain dates, population, method and limits where relevant.

First distinguish describing supplied observations, assessing a causal explanation,
inventing a hypothesis, designing a test and interpreting actual results. Choose the
matching operation rather than routing every scientific question into an experiment.
Creative hypothesis generation can be useful, but the resulting hypotheses remain
separate from findings. Use existing sufficient evidence before proposing new data
collection; a material unresolved question can justify a discriminating test.

Useful methods: hypothesis comparison, counterexamples, evidence mapping and targeted retrieval. Apply the research-and-writing skill for substantive synthesis and drafts. An internally consistent explanation is not an externally verified result.

Output: bounded conclusion, competing explanations, source support and unresolved questions. A research plan is not completed research. A proposed scientific mechanism is not an experimental finding.

## Design an informative experiment

Use when one uncertain assumption determines whether to pursue the idea. Specify hypothesis, target population/system, stimulus/intervention, observable behavior, baseline/control where feasible, success/failure interpretation and resulting next decision. Identify confounds and operational constraints.

Choose a method appropriate to available evidence and resources: interview, observation, paper prototype, concierge trial, engineering bench test, field pilot or controlled comparison. A few qualitative observations can reveal a failure mode without establishing a population effect. Do not fabricate sample-size calculations, significance or certainty.

Output: executable test protocol with measurement and decision rules. Label it planned until actually run; record unexpected results as observations before explaining them.

### Continue when results arrive

Recover the tested assumption and original observation/decision rule from the supplied
plan or authorized project record. Inspect actual results before interpreting them.
Separate observations from explanations; check material confounds, missing comparisons
and whether the evidence is inconclusive. Disclose any changed measure or threshold
rather than judging the original test against a new rule.

Explain what the evidence changes and recommend proceeding, adjusting, stopping or
gathering more evidence within delegated authority. Preserve user-selected commitments
unless the user changes them. Do not treat a favorable anecdote as established demand,
an unrun protocol as results, or an inconclusive trial as proof of failure.
Keep this as small as the job permits; it is not a mandatory experiment template.

If saving is requested, reuse the existing project record's note and next_step events
with source references. Do not create a new storage system or claim durable recall
from ordinary conversation context.

## Redesign a service or process

Use for handoffs, queues, coordination, resource constraints and recurring operational friction. Map the current sequence and owners, including exception paths. Identify who gains and who may inherit extra cost or burden. Preserve essential controls and access needs.

Useful methods: subtraction, resequencing, bottleneck analysis, feedback design and incentive changes. Address implementation dependencies and the smallest reversible rollout. A smoother happy path is insufficient if routine exceptions become unmanageable.

Output: current versus proposed flow, ownership, exception handling, expected mechanism of improvement, implementation steps and a measured pilot. User-visible benefits and operational measurements must remain distinguishable.

## Everyday and personal problem solving

Use for routines, learning, organization and constrained practical decisions. Respect the person's actual energy, resources and preferences. Diagnose the point of friction before suggesting a complicated system. Prefer a small reversible intervention when it can answer the question cheaply.

Useful methods: subtraction, environmental redesign, sequencing, tradeoff comparison and short experiments. Do not medicalize an ordinary productivity issue or offer unwarranted certainty in high-stakes domains; use the host's applicable guidance and verification requirements.

Output: a usable next action or small plan with a clear check of whether it helps. No need for a product launch, audience study or elaborate concept deck unless requested.

## Across all playbooks

The conductor controls transitions. New evidence can invalidate the current explanation, a new constraint can eliminate a candidate, and a precise user request can narrow the operation. Preserve unaffected work. A stage is complete when its requested output and acceptance conditions are satisfied, not when every named method has been performed.
