# Innovation strategy

Turn the actual problem into a useful decision, mechanism, intervention or test. Innovation can mean a simpler process, a better incentive, a different business model, a scientific hypothesis or a practical daily workaround. It does not require a visual asset or story.

Apply `../creative-director/references/studio-router.md` and the conductor's current task contract. Read only the relevant sections of `../../references/innovation-playbooks.md`. Methods selected by the conductor govern this pass; do not restart diagnosis or stack more methods merely to sound thorough.

## Establish the working problem

Use the user's existing context to identify the desired change, affected people/system, current outcome, constraints, available evidence and requested deliverable. Distinguish a symptom from a known cause, a solution request from a demonstrated need, and a preference from an invariant. Ask only when an unresolved choice would materially change the work; otherwise label assumptions and proceed.

Identify the type of uncertainty that currently limits progress:

- Goal uncertainty: which outcome or stakeholder tradeoff matters?
- Causal uncertainty: what produces the observed result?
- Possibility uncertainty: what mechanisms or alternatives could work?
- Decision uncertainty: which option best fits the actual criteria?
- Execution uncertainty: what dependencies, capabilities or failure modes remain?

These are diagnosis aids, not mandatory stages. A task may already have sufficient answers or request one narrow piece.

Apply the recognition playbook's proportionality policy: necessary investigation and
checking follow uncertainty, dependencies and consequence; optional breadth follows
the user's budget; requested length follows presentation. One short high-stakes answer
may need careful checks. A simple calculation does not need an innovation tournament.

## Perform the selected operation

- **Frame:** express the problem as an outcome with constraints; examine whether the stated boundary hides the real bottleneck. Preserve the user's intended goal rather than substituting an easier problem.
- **Calculate or reason formally:** select the relevant quantitative or formal-claim procedure in ../../references/innovation-playbooks.md. Establish definitions, inputs and assumptions, perform the operation and check the result. Do not treat uncertain adoption or a causal explanation as an exact calculation.
- **Explain or coach:** use the corresponding playbook operation. The requested answer, question or feedback is the deliverable; do not replace a learning interaction with a solved artifact.
- **Diagnose:** map the process or causal hypotheses, inspect actual evidence, consider alternative explanations and identify an observation that would distinguish them. A plausible root cause is still a hypothesis until tested.
- **Invent:** generate alternatives that change the causal mechanism, arrangement, incentive, sequence, ownership or resource use. Use the requested count and preserve hard constraints. Separate a useful analogy from proof that it transfers.
- **Decide:** compare feasible options against the user's objectives and constraints. Show the decisive tradeoff and sensitivity to uncertain assumptions. Recommend or select only to the extent the user delegated that decision.
- **Develop:** turn the selected direction into a coherent operating model: who does what, with which inputs, through which mechanism, producing which outcome. Address the consequential edge cases and dependencies.
- **Test:** select the smallest informative test of the most consequential uncertain assumption. Specify the observation and resulting decision before seeing results.
- **Execute or hand off:** create the requested artifact or use the available host tools within authorization. A plan does not substitute for implementation when implementation is requested and possible.

Use `../research-and-writing/SKILL.md` for substantive source synthesis or evidence-led writing; use `../artifact-quality/SKILL.md` to inspect the requested result with its matching rubric. Feedback can activate `../creative-learning/SKILL.md` within its scope.

Assign procedures to the phase they support. A mixed assignment may need empirical
diagnosis, creative alternatives, quantitative feasibility and implementation checks;
completing one does not satisfy the others. Include only necessary phases, preserve
accepted work and stop when the requested outcome is sufficiently supported or a
concrete evidence/tool limit controls the conclusion.

## Make ideas concrete

For each serious candidate, capture only what is useful at the requested depth:

`mechanism; intended beneficiary/outcome; required assumptions; constraints satisfied; principal tradeoff; failure condition; next informative observation`

This is a working contract, not a required public template for every response. A quick request may need one clear paragraph. Distinct names, colors or adjectives do not make the same mechanism into different ideas. Conversely, a modest change with a defensible benefit can be more useful than an extravagant novelty.

Check how the proposal could move costs, risk or workload elsewhere in the system. Surface consequential stakeholder conflicts and second-order effects. Use baseline values only when supplied or measured; label estimates and ranges. Do not invent ROI, adoption, experimental results, engineering feasibility or customer demand.

## Stop and deliver appropriately

Stop divergent work when the requested alternatives exist or further exploration adds no useful difference. Stop diagnosis when the evidence supports the requested conclusion, or state the unresolved competing explanations. Do not force certainty to close a task.

Deliver the requested decision memo, causal analysis, options, operating model, experiment, prototype or handoff. Include the decisive rationale, material uncertainty and next action when needed. Check the artifact against the brief and applicable rubric. Distinguish proposed, implemented, observed and validated results.

## Examples

- "Our support queue keeps growing; find a practical fix" → inspect arrival/completion/rework evidence, diagnose the limiting mechanism, develop an intervention and measurable trial. Do not begin with a new brand concept.
- "Invent five business models for this unused equipment" → identify constraints, vary the value/ownership/revenue mechanisms, compare assumptions and suggest cheap demand tests.
- "Which of these three designs should we build?" → constraints and tradeoffs, sensitivity to uncertain inputs, recommendation or delegated selection. Do not reopen ideation by default.
- "Make school pickup less chaotic with no extra staff" → map people, timing and handoffs; try removal, sequencing or incentive changes; protect safety constraints and specify a bounded test.
- "Could a different physical principle solve this engineering bottleneck?" → separate requirements from current implementation assumptions, explore transferable mechanisms, and mark technical evidence still needed.

## Commitment-specific candidate decisions

When a consequential comparison benefits from an explicit evidence contract, read
../creative-director/references/candidate-decisions.md. Judge readiness for the next
specific commitment, not an entire venture at once. A hypothesis can justify a small
test while remaining insufficient for a rollout. Hard constraints cannot be averaged
away by attractive ratings. A single practical option can be sufficient; do not
invent a tournament or scores. Pair proposed tests with later observations and
revisit the affected decision; preserve still-valid work and existing authorization.
