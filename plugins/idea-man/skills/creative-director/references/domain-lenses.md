# Domain Lenses

The creative constitution is written in advertising language (brand, audience, client).
Translate relevant criteria to the actual deliverable. Read only needed lenses; a
brand music-video treatment may use C and G. A lens is not a genre prescription.
Treat historical anchors as illustrative judgments, not predictions or verified outcomes
for an unmade proposal. The "What 7 means here" passages explain an optional scale;
they do not establish a presentation bar.

Below, craft checks become hard gates only when the actual brief makes them requirements.
A missing observation is unknown, not a failed test. Do not claim children, customers or
players passed a check unless they were actually observed; simulated perspectives are
hypotheses. Preserve requested options with weaknesses instead of automatically killing
unfamiliar forms, documentary stories, abstract animation or deliberately niche work.

## 0. Hook vs engine

Distinguish initial attraction (hook) from repeatable value (engine) when the format
needs both. Explain the concrete mechanism and evidence behind each; optional scores
are not measurements of future pull. A one-off image, film or intervention may succeed
without a repeatable engine. Do not require a hook score of 7 or promise retention from
a compelling premise. Use historical comparisons to ask better questions, not to
declare an untested proposal a hit or failure.

## A. Film / TV / streaming

**What 7 means here:** a logline a development exec would ask to read the script for — ironic, picturable, with a clear engine or transformation — even if it's not yet *Jaws*.

**Criteria**
1. One-line clarity / high concept — the idea fits in about 25 words (Spielberg, per
   Wyatt's *High Concept*); "the idea is king" (Katzenberg's 1991 memo).
2. Irony — the hero is the worst possible person for this situation (Snyder, *Save the Cat*).
3. A compelling mental picture — the poster sells itself.
4. Audience and cost are legible; the title says what it is (Snyder).
5. Story, not situation — the protagonist ends changed and there is a moral dimension;
   a situation is a puzzle with an obvious solution (Jeff Lyons).
6. A controlling idea — a specific, truthful cause-and-effect statement about the world (McKee).
7. Stakes and necessity — "What are the stakes?" / "Why must you tell THIS story?"
   (Pixar's story rules, Emma Coats).
8. Engine — series: a premise that generates endless storylines, or a finite
   transformation clock ("Mr. Chips to Scarface", Gilligan on *Breaking Bad*).

**Craft checks**
- Poster test: describe the one image on the one-sheet. Can't → no high concept.
- Wrong-man test: replace the hero with a generic competent person. Nothing lost → no irony.
- Situation test: does solving the problem change the hero, or only end the plot?
- Episode-47 test (series): ten episode loglines in ten minutes without bending the premise.
- Promise-of-the-premise: five set pieces only this premise can produce.

**Anchors**
- 10: *Jaws* (primal fear, contained arena, ironic hero, self-selling poster);
  *Groundhog Day* (a situation that forces moral transformation; the title became an idiom).
- 9: *Get Out* (a controlling idea about racism delivered as genre; tiny budget, huge
  return, Oscar for original screenplay); *Back to the Future* (would I have been friends
  with my teenage parent? — maximal irony); *Breaking Bad*, *Severance* (the work/life
  split made literal — a metaphor engine for seasons); *Inside Out*, *The Truman Show*.
- 8: *Squid Game* (death-game lineage lifted by children's games + debt); *Up* (a 10-level
  opening; a premise whose second half drifts from its controlling idea).
- 4–5: *FlashForward* (great hook, no engine); *Bright* (two templates combined; each half
  fails the swap test).

**Translation:** brand → the show / the protagonist's flaw · one sentence → logline of ≤25
words (protagonist, goal, ironic obstacle) · surprise → irony or a what-if inversion ·
audience recognises themselves → recognises the fear or longing · swap test → genre swap
(put the genre default where the hook is; if the story survives, it's a template) ·
memorability → title, poster, one quotable scene · scalability → world rules for sequels or
an episode engine.

**For story work, also apply** any story taste profile the user keeps (e.g. a
taste-profile skill) as an extra lens, and its anti-patterns as scoped criteria when the user made them requirements.

## B. Animation & kids' content

**What 7 means here:** a character + engine a kid would act out at recess and a parent would stay on the couch for; ten episodes come easily.

**Criteria**
1. A lead with a comic flaw or an innocence archetype (SpongeBob as the Laurel-style
   innocent among irritable straight men).
2. A repeatable engine; formula as a feature (*Phineas and Ferb*: the build is the
   setting, three plotlines converge, a song and a chase every episode).
3. Co-viewing: the parent genuinely wants to watch (Joe Brumm on *Bluey*).
4. Kid truth: the engine comes from how kids actually behave (kids hate it when games stop).
5. Format and platform fit (7-minute *Bluey* episodes; 2–3-minute song units on YouTube,
   iterated on audience data).
6. Proof of pull (the *Adventure Time* short went viral before the series).
7. Developmental soundness — engagement metrics can beat developmental value; don't let them.
8. Visual read — silhouette and drawability.

**Craft checks** (apply only to the requested audience, format and requirements)
- Who's the star? For character-led work, identify the lead or duo a child could ask for by name. A format,
  a narrator or abstract format may serve a different brief; inspect intended engagement
  rather than rejecting it for lacking a conventional star.
- Playground test: can kids act it out at recess with no props?
- Parent-stays test: does the parent stay on the couch?
- 10-in-10: ten episode premises in ten minutes from the character alone.
- Scribble test: can a six-year-old draw the lead from memory?
- Lesson-strip test: remove the moral. Nothing funny or compelling left → homework.

**Anchors**
- 10: *Bluey* (the engine is play itself; parents see themselves in Bandit); *SpongeBob*.
- 9: *Phineas and Ferb* (rigid formula, endless variation — pitched for 16 years as "too
  complex", so "too complex" is not a valid kill); *Peppa Pig* (radical simplicity, travels
  globally).
- 8: *Adventure Time* (a world and tone rather than a formula).
- 8 commercial / ~5 creative: *CoComelon* — score engine and originality separately.
- 4: loud randomness standing in for character truth (e.g. *Breadwinners*); IP-first toy
  tie-ins where the brand exists and the engine doesn't.

**Translation:** brand → the lead's flaw or desire · one sentence → character + engine
("every summer day two brothers build the impossible while their sister tries to bust
them") · audience recognises themselves → kids see their own play, parents their own
parenting · surprise → the broken beat inside the formula · swap test → flaw swap (if the
lead could be any nice kid, fail) · memorability → catchphrase, silhouette, theme song ·
scalability → 52-episode engine, clippability for Shorts, toyetic world.

## C. Music video, songs & artist concepts

**What 7 means here:** one rule or image you could describe in six words that only works for this song/artist, makeable with the real tools and budget.

**Criteria**
1. One rule / one image (OK Go "Here It Goes Again": treadmills, one take, cheap).
2. Structural lock to the song — the video is the arrangement made visible (Gondry's
   "Star Guitar", "Come Into My World").
3. Contrast that carries meaning (Childish Gambino "This Is America": joyous dance against
   violence; the music flips at the first gunshot).
4. Persona as concept (Sia hiding her face; Daft Punk; Gorillaz as a manufactured band).
5. Participation / meme-ability (Lil Nas X built "Old Town Road" as a meme before it charted).
6. Decode / rewatch density (Kendrick Lamar "HUMBLE." tableaux).
7. Hook craft for the song itself — melodic symmetry ("melodic math") serves psychology,
   not arithmetic.
8. Prediction humility — audio features don't predict hits (Pachet & Roy 2008) and social
   influence makes success unpredictable (Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006). Judge the concept;
   never promise a hit.

**Craft checks**
- Screenshot test: is any single frame identifiable as THIS video?
- Song-swap test: what is lost on a different track? A shared visual device may still
  work; inspect whether this brief needs song-specific structure.
- Text-a-friend test: a fan can describe it in ~six words.
- Recreate test: can fans copy it with a phone (dance, challenge, costume)?
- Mute test: does it hold with the sound off in feed autoplay?

**Anchors**
- 10: "This Is America"; OK Go "Here It Goes Again"; Gondry's "Come Into My World" /
  "Star Guitar".
- 9: Sia "Chandelier" (a persona problem solved with one performer in one room);
  Beyoncé *Lemonade* (a format idea: visual album in chapters); Daft Punk / Gorillaz at
  the artist level.
- 8: "HUMBLE." (iconic tableaux, but a montage rather than one rule).
- 9 release concept / ~6 video: "Old Town Road".
- 4–5: band in a moody warehouse + breakup narrative; an AI persona with no human truth
  behind it (FN Meka, dropped by its label within days in 2022) — directly relevant to
  AI-artist projects.

**Translation:** brand → artist + this song · one sentence → the video's rule · surprise →
the never-seen image or a "how did they do that?" · emotional specificity → the lyric's
feeling made literal · swap test → song swap + artist swap · memorability → screenshot, GIF,
dance step · scalability → recreatable challenge, extension across an album era, translation
to the live show.

**AI production reality (feasibility gate):** if the work will be made with generative
tools (video/image/music models), favour ideas whose power survives imperfect
consistency: one rule, one location, strong graphic idea, stylised worlds, contrast and
editing concepts. Ideas that need long continuous photoreal performance or exact
choreography are expensive in that pipeline.

## D. Product / software / startup

**What 7 means here:** clearly better than what users do now (including the dumb free workaround) at the specific struggling moment, so a real segment would change behaviour; it does not need to be iPhone-scale. A sound, insight-driven feature that moves the core metric is a 7.

**Criteria**
1. Organic, not made-up — founders want it, can build it, others don't see it; avoid
   "sitcom" ideas (Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas").
2. Well, not wide — a small number of people want it a large amount (Graham).
3. Urgency — people would use a crappy v1; hair-on-fire problems make demand obvious
   (Sequoia's PMF archetypes).
4. Live in the future, then build what's missing; schlep blindness leaves tedious ideas
   uncrowded (Graham; Stripe).
5. Much better than the closest substitute, including the dumbest free one.
6. Job to be done — what is it hired to replace? (the milkshake vs bagel/doughnut).
7. PR/FAQ "so what?" — fails if not meaningfully better for the customer (Amazon).
8. Kano — today's delighters become tomorrow's must-haves; post-launch, disappointment surveys can investigate dependence, with population and
   sampling limits. No universal percentage proves fit for this project.

**Craft checks**
- Hand-squeeze test: name the dumbest free substitute. Clearly better than that?
- Early-adoption check: what usable minimum would someone try, and what behavior would
  reveal value? Do not treat simulated enthusiasm as observed willingness.
- Dependence check: inspect actual evidence of who relies on the product. A pre-launch
  idea may have no users yet; identify a useful demand test instead of inventing people.
- So-what headline: would a customer forward the PR headline?
- Competitor-logo swap: what is specific to this product? Shared effective practices
  need not be novel; investigate the actual user advantage.

**Anchors**
- 10: iPhone (three devices in one; multitouch delighter became a must-have); Figma
  (browser + real-time multiplayer for design).
- 9: Airbnb (air mattresses during a sold-out conference); Uber (tap for a car).
- 8: Slack (organic, born as an internal tool of a failed game); Spotify Wrapped and
  Duolingo streaks at feature level (users become distribution; habit mechanics).
- 6–7: BeReal (novel constraint, failed retention).
- 4: Google Glass (tech in search of a job, social cost); Quibi (free substitutes were
  better).
- 2–3: Juicero (squeezing the pack by hand worked — the substitute beat the product).

**Translation:** brand → the core action · one sentence → the PR headline · audience
recognises themselves → recognises their own workaround · surprise → a non-obvious insight
plus a delighter · simplicity → one core action · emotional specificity → the functional,
emotional and social job · swap test → competitor-logo swap · memorability → a
word-of-mouth sentence or shareable artifact · scalability → network effects, growth
loops, path from well to wide.

## E. Games

**What 7 means here:** a core verb and loop that's fun in greybox, with real decisions and something players would retell.

**Criteria**
1. Interesting decisions — no dominant strategy (Sid Meier).
2. Rewards the first and the hundredth play (Bushnell's Law); familiarity, not just
   simplicity, makes it learnable (Bogost).
3. Multi-problem elegance — a good idea solves several problems at once (Miyamoto).
4. Lateral thinking with withered technology (Yokoi; Game Boy beat better-specced rivals).
5. Find the fun in a cheap prototype before production.
6. Constraint as hook + share artifact (Wordle: one puzzle a day; the emoji grid).
7. Watchability / social loop (Among Us on a Mafia structure; spread via streamers).
8. Ethical retention (Flappy Bird's creator pulled it over addictiveness).

**Craft checks**
- Greybox test: fun with cubes and no art?
- Dominant-strategy test: always-best move → fake decisions.
- One-verb test: name the core verb (jump, portal, place).
- 30-second loop test: is the smallest repeated action satisfying on its own?
- Watch test: fun to watch someone else play?

**Anchors**
- 10: Tetris; Minecraft (two verbs, infinite player-authored goals).
- 9: Portal (one mechanic generates every puzzle and the story); Pokémon GO (IP + real world
  + walking; then a steep retention drop until features rebuilt the loop).
- 8: Wordle (elegant constraint, thin mastery ceiling); Among Us (proven decision structure;
  discovery timing ≠ idea quality).
- 5: Flappy Bird (viral ≠ great; timing-only frustration, no decisions).
- 4: Concord (competent, fails the swap test against its genre leader; shut down weeks
  after launch).

**Translation:** brand → the core verb · one sentence → "You [verb] to [goal] but [tension]"
· surprise → emergent, unscripted moments · simplicity → learnable through familiarity ·
emotional specificity → the loop's feeling (dread, mastery, betrayal) · swap test →
mechanic swap · memorability → stories players tell, clips · scalability → systemic content,
UGC, multiplayer, live ops.

## F. Engineering / operations / business problems

**What 7 means here:** the fix sits at the root cause, is hard to game, and is reversible or cheap to test; elegance (one move solving several problems) pushes it higher.

**Criteria**
1. Root cause, not symptom (Ohno's five whys — useful, though too basic alone for deep causes).
2. Problem framing — is there a better problem to solve? (Wedell-Wedellsborg, HBR 2017).
3. Multi-problem elegance (one move fixes several things).
4. Cost vs impact (containerisation cut loading cost from about $5.86 to $0.16 a ton and
   cut theft and time too).
5. Reversibility — one-way doors need care, two-way doors should be fast (Bezos 2016 letter).
6. Second-order effects and incentives — how will people game it? (Hanoi's rat-tail bounty
   bred rats; the Delhi cobra story is likely apocryphal, so don't cite it).
7. Constraint-native simplicity (Apollo 13's improvised CO₂ adapter from on-board materials).
8. Single points of failure; pre-mortem.

**Craft checks**
- Which-why test: does the fix sit at why #1 (symptom) or why #4–5?
- Exploit test: how would a smart, self-interested actor game it?
- Remove-the-patch test: pull the fix — does the problem return at once? Then it's a patch.
- Single-point test: what one failure kills it?
- Door test: if wrong, can it be undone by Friday?

**Anchors**
- 10: containerisation; Apollo 13's adapter.
- 9: Toyota's andon / five-whys culture (problems surfaced at the source).
- 7–8: mirrors beside slow elevators (cheap, reversible reframe — right only if perception
  was the real problem).
- 3–4: Boeing MCAS (a software patch over an aerodynamic change, relying on one sensor);
  Hanoi rat bounty (rewarded the metric, not the outcome).

**Translation:** brand → the system or organisation · one sentence → "The problem is X
because Y; we change Z" · surprise → a reframe · simplicity → fewest moving parts ·
emotional specificity → a named stakeholder's pain and how often it happens · swap test →
generic-remedy check ("more training", "a dashboard", "hire someone" fix nothing
specific) · memorability → becomes standard practice · scalability → standardisation and
compounding.

## G. Brand / advertising — what is different

**What 7 means here:** Leo Burnett's "Inspiring" — a human-centred idea that matters to people, beautifully crafted — judged against the best work at this project's scale and budget.

- An ad must earn attention it didn't ask for; films, games and products are chosen.
- Brand linkage is unique to ads; the swap test is native here.
- One single-minded proposition; series and games reward layered depth.
- Success is measured outside the idea (brand or behaviour change).
- Campaigns are finite; series, products and games need engines.
- The HumanKind and craft scales are native to this lens; calibration anchors are in
  `creative-constitution.md`.

## H. Names, titles, taglines

**What 7 means here:** sayable, ownable, and it either says what it is or makes you ask — with a second layer an insider would feel.

**Criteria:** says what it is or makes you ask (one, not neither); sayable aloud on first
read; ownable (search it — is it taken in this category?); carries the insight or the
irony; survives translation and doesn't mean something embarrassing elsewhere; short.

**Craft checks:** the phone test (say it on a call — can they spell it?); the competitor test
(could a rival use it?); the cringe test (would the target audience say it out loud?).

**Method note:** generate in families (descriptive, evocative, invented, metaphor,
collision of two words, borrowed from a far domain) when they add meaningful differences.
Preserve requested count; stop when further names repeat the same naming mechanism.
Search/translation checks are unverified until actually performed and are not legal clearance.

## I. Visual / design concepts

**What 7 means here:** a clear dominant idea, with a generative rule when the brief needs
a system. A strong single image need not become a fifty-application identity.

**Criteria:** clear dominant idea at the intended viewing scale; when a visual system is
requested, test whether its rule survives varied applications; reduction — every element earns its place (Swiss/
Bauhaus); a signature move a competitor couldn't copy without looking derivative; works at
thumbnail size and at billboard size.

**Craft checks:** squint test (does the hierarchy hold blurred?); logo-off test (still
recognisable as this brand/project?); 50th-application test (sketch five more uses).

For UI and web builds, hand the chosen concept to the user's design-direction skill if one
exists.

## J. Calibration warnings — read before scoring

1. **Gatekeeper rejection is not decisive evidence.** *Back to the Future* was rejected about 40
   times; *Squid Game* waited about a decade; *Phineas and Ferb* was pitched for 16 years
   as too complex; Among Us sat near-empty for two years. Never kill an idea for being too
   weird, complex or strange. Inspect actual constraints and evidence; an optional craft
   exercise is not a universal gate.
2. **Virality is not greatness.** Score flash and engine separately (Flappy Bird, BeReal).
3. **A commercial engine is not a creative idea** (CoComelon).
4. **Never claim to predict hits.** Judge the concept and its mechanism.
5. **Judge against the real arena.** A neighbourhood café, an indie artist or a three-person app
   is judged against the best work of its size and budget, not against global award winners.
   Global anchors calibrate the top of the scale; they are not the bar for a 7.
