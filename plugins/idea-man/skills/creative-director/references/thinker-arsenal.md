# Thinker Arsenal

Seventeen thinking modes in six families. Each mode is defined by the cognitive trap it
defeats, so casting is a diagnosis: name the trap the problem is setting, then send in the
mode that beats it. Every method card gives the move, the procedure to actually run, and a
true story that has been fact-checked. Stories marked *parable* or *lore* are useful
illustrations whose details are not documented; say so if you cite them.

## How to cast

**Rules**
1. Three to five methods per round, from at least three different modes, spanning at least
   two families.
2. Always include: one **distance** method (L4 Provoke or L5 Connect), one **subtract or
   invert** method (L2/L3), and one **domain-native** method (Craft modes for creative
   work; Systems or Human modes for products and operations).
3. A persona alone ("think like Steve Jobs") barely changes LLM output; a persona with its
   procedure does. Always run the procedure.
4. Justify each casting in one line tied to the Phase 1 trap.

**Domain leanings** (starting points, not rules)
| Domain | Lean on |
|---|---|
| Film / series premise | C1, H3, L4, L2, C2 (series engine) |
| Kids' animation | C2, H1, C1, S3 |
| Music video / artist concept | C1 (image), S3, H3, L5, C2 (track-and-hook for songs) |
| Advertising / brand | H3, S1 (convention audit), L2 (flaw-flip), H2, L4 |
| Names / titles / taglines | D1 (quota), L5, H3 (DHM), H2 (SUCCESs) |
| Product / software / startup | H1, L1, L3, S2, D2 |
| Games | C2 (MDA, Sid's rules), S2 (multi-problem), S3 (withered tech) |
| Engineering / ops / business | Y2, Y1, S2, L3, L1 |

**The user's three archetypes** map onto families: *Lateral thinkers* (de Bono) → LATERAL;
*Systems thinkers* (Bose, Gordon Murray) → SYSTEMS; *Divergent thinkers* (Edison) →
DIVERGENT. STRUCTURAL, HUMAN and CRAFT extend the room.

# LATERAL family — break the pattern

## L1 REFRAME
**Trap:** solving the problem exactly as stated.

#### How-Might-We ladder — Min Basadur, Procter & Gamble (early 1970s)
- **Move:** the stated problem is usually a solution in disguise; climb to a better question.
- **Procedure:** 1) Restate the brief as "How might we…". 2) Ladder up: ask "Why do we want
  this?" and rewrite the answer as a broader HMW (2–3 times). 3) Ladder down: ask "What's
  stopping us?" and turn each obstacle into a narrower HMW. 4) Choose a rung one level above
  the obvious — wide enough for several answers, narrow enough to name a user and a benefit.
  5) Generate against two or three rungs.
- **Story:** P&G's copies of Irish Spring's green-striped soap kept losing. "How can we make
  a better green-stripe bar?" became "How might we create a more refreshing soap of our
  own?" — which led to Coast.
- **Use for:** any brief that is really "make a better X".

#### Question Burst — Hal Gregersen (HBR, 2018)
- **Move:** questions, not answers, unlock a stuck problem.
- **Procedure:** 1) State the challenge in under two minutes. 2) For four minutes generate
  only questions — no preambles, no answers — at least 15. 3) Flag the ones that challenge an
  assumption or open a new angle. 4) Turn the best three into HMWs. AI version: simulate 3–5
  different askers (customer, competitor, child, regulator, artist).
- **Use for:** unclear briefs, stuck projects.

#### Predatory Thinking — Dave Trott (2013)
- **Move:** get upstream of the problem; change the contest instead of competing harder.
- **Procedure:** 1) State the challenge as given. 2) Ask what would have to change for it to
  become easy. 3) Redefine the opponent or the scoreboard. 4) Solve the restated problem.
- **Story (Trott's parable):** two explorers hear a tiger; one laces up running shoes — he
  doesn't need to outrun the tiger, only the other explorer.
- **Use for:** low-budget ads, positioning, pitches, competitive strategy.

#### Find the real bottleneck — Paul MacCready (Gossamer Condor, 1977)
- **Move:** the hard-looking problem hides a different, solvable one.
- **Procedure:** 1) List why everyone else is failing. 2) Ask what limits the *rate of
  learning* (iterations, tests, feedback), not just the result. 3) Redesign for that limit.
- **Story:** the Kremer Prize for human-powered flight was set in 1959 (raised to £50,000 in
  1973) and stood unclaimed for 18 years. Rivals' elaborate aircraft took months to rebuild
  after crashes; MacCready built a plane from Mylar, aluminium tubing and wire that could be
  repaired in hours, so his team could test far more often. Bryan Allen flew it to the prize
  on 23 August 1977. (The repair-time framing comes from Aza Raskin's 2011 retelling.)
- **Use for:** production pipelines, R&D, any "we keep failing" problem.

#### Double-loop learning — Chris Argyris & Donald Schön (1978)
- **Move:** question the goal or rule that produces the actions, not just the actions.
- **Procedure:** 1) Name the governing variable (target, KPI, belief). 2) Ask whether it is
  the right one. 3) Compare what people say they value with what they actually do.
- **Analogy:** a thermostat that adjusts heat is single-loop; one that asks whether 20°C is
  the right temperature is double-loop.
- **Use for:** teams optimising the wrong metric; content chasing the wrong KPI.

#### Wicked problems & issue mapping — Horst Rittel & Melvin Webber (1973); IBIS (Kunz & Rittel 1970); Jeff Conklin's dialogue mapping (2006)
- **Move:** for open-ended problems, formulating the problem *is* the work.
- **Procedure:** map the problem as Questions → Ideas → Arguments (pro/con), let new questions
  emerge from the arguments, and decide only when the map stabilises.
- **Use for:** messy multi-stakeholder problems, world-building rules, strategy.

## L2 INVERT
**Trap:** reasoning only forward, from what you want or what you can see.

#### Inversion — Carl Jacobi ("man muss immer umkehren": invert, always invert), popularised by Charlie Munger
- **Procedure:** 1) Ask "How would we guarantee this fails / is boring / is ignored?"
  2) List every way. 3) Invert each into a design rule or an idea.
- **Use for:** everything; especially when forward brainstorming stalls.

#### Look at what's missing — Abraham Wald and survivorship bias
- **Move:** the data you can see was filtered; the answer may live in what's absent.
- **Procedure:** 1) Ask what never reaches you (the users who left, the projects that died,
  the audience that never clicked). 2) Design for that silent group.
- **Story (careful):** Wald's 1943 Statistical Research Group memos estimated aircraft
  vulnerability from damage on planes that *returned*. The popular "armour where the holes
  aren't" dialogue and diagrams are later reconstructions — cite the principle, not the scene.

#### Flaw-flip — Bill Bernbach / DDB: Volkswagen "Think Small" and "Lemon" (1959); Avis "We Try Harder" (1962)
- **Procedure:** 1) List the weaknesses the category hides. 2) Pick the one the audience
  already believes. 3) Say it plainly. 4) Show the benefit the weakness causes ("small, so it's
  cheap and parks anywhere"; "No. 2, so we try harder"). 5) Make the style as candid as the claim.
- **Story:** Ad Age named the VW work the best campaign of the 20th century; Avis's line ran
  for about 50 years.
- **Use for:** challengers, underdog artists, low-budget productions, honest brands.

#### Reframe the failure — Spencer Silver & Art Fry (3M, Post-it Note)
- **Procedure:** 1) Take the failed result or side effect. 2) Ask who would want exactly that
  property. 3) Build around it.
- **Story:** Silver's 1968 adhesive was too weak to be "glue". Fry wanted a bookmark for his
  hymnal that wouldn't tear the page (1973 or 1974, accounts differ). Test-marketed as "Press
  'n Peel" in 1977; launched as Post-it Notes in 1980.
- **Use for:** happy accidents, AI-generation glitches, rejected drafts.

#### List what wouldn't happen next — Pixar story rule #9 (Emma Coats, 2011)
- **Procedure:** when stuck, list what *wouldn't* happen next; the material to break the
  block is often on that list.

## L3 SUBTRACT
**Trap:** the addition habit — solving by adding features, scenes, words, parts.

#### Subtraction neglect — Adams, Converse, Hales & Klotz (*Nature*, 2021); via negativa — Nassim Taleb (*Antifragile*, 2012)
- **Finding:** people systematically overlook subtractive changes, especially under load.
- **Procedure:** before adding anything, list five things to remove and test each removal.

#### Remove the core feature — Sony Walkman (1979)
- **Procedure:** 1) List the features the category considers essential. 2) Delete the one
  everyone assumes. 3) See what new category the remainder creates.
- **Story:** Masaru Ibuka wanted to listen to music on long flights. Sony's team turned the
  Pressman cassette recorder into a playback-only stereo unit — recording and speaker removed
  — and Akio Morita pushed it to market for young buyers. The TPS-L2 launched 1 July 1979.

#### Five-step engineering algorithm — as publicly described by Elon Musk (2021)
- **Procedure (never reorder):** 1) Make each requirement less dumb — attach a person's name
  to it and question it. 2) Delete parts and steps (if you aren't adding back ~10%, you didn't
  delete enough). 3) Simplify what survives. 4) Accelerate the cycle. 5) Automate last.
- **Use for:** workflows, software features, production and crew processes.

#### Simplify, then add lightness — William Stout's credo ("simplicate and add more lightness"), adopted by Colin Chapman at Lotus
- **Move:** every part must earn its weight. Chapman's corollary is usually given as: adding
  power makes you faster on the straights; subtracting weight makes you faster everywhere.
- **Use for:** product design, scripts, edits, decks.

#### Less, but better — Dieter Rams ("Weniger, aber besser")
- **Procedure:** strip until removing one more element would break the idea; then refine
  what's left.
- **Use for:** visual design, product, titles, UI.

## L4 PROVOKE
**Trap:** vertical logic and mental set — each step follows "sensibly" from the last.

#### Lateral thinking — Edward de Bono (1967)
- **Move:** escape the dominant pattern deliberately instead of digging deeper in it.
- **Procedure:** name the dominant idea and the assumptions holding it in place; challenge
  each ("why must it be this way?"); generate alternatives from outside the pattern.

#### Po (provocative operation) — Edward de Bono (*The Mechanism of Mind*, 1969)
- **Procedure:** 1) State a deliberately impossible or wrong provocation, flagged "Po".
  2) Don't judge it — *move* from it: extract a principle, a moment, a difference, a benefit.
  3) Follow the movement to a practical idea.
- **Story:** de Bono's own example: "Po, cars have square wheels." Moving from it (what
  would square wheels make the car do?) leads toward suspension that anticipates the bumps.
- **Provocation types:** reversal, exaggeration, distortion, wishful thinking, escape (drop
  something taken for granted).

#### Random entry — Edward de Bono
- **Procedure:** 1) Draw a truly random word (`scripts/draw.py --only words`). 2) List its
  properties and associations. 3) Force a connection from each to the problem. 4) Keep the
  connection that yields a new direction.
- **Illustration (de Bono):** "nose" + photocopier → a machine that releases a scent when it
  is running out of paper.

#### Oblique Strategies — Brian Eno & Peter Schmidt (1975)
- **Move:** an arbitrary instruction breaks the session's rut; obey it literally.
- **Procedure:** draw a card (use `scripts/draw.py` — its deck is original prompts in the
  same spirit; don't reproduce the published deck), obey it for one pass, keep whatever it
  shakes loose.
- **Story:** used on Bowie's *Low*, *"Heroes"* and *Lodger*; Coldplay reportedly used them on
  *Viva la Vida* (2008). Real cards include "Honor thy error as a hidden intention".

#### Creative Paradox — Gordon MacKenzie (Hallmark; *Orbiting the Giant Hairball*, 1998)
- **Move:** inside a big organisation, absurd briefs and inverted constraints flush out
  groupthink. MacKenzie spent about 30 years at Hallmark and gave himself the title "Creative
  Paradox".
- **Procedure:** invert the brief (the opposite occasion, the wrong audience, the forbidden
  tone) and see which truths it exposes.

#### Explorer / Artist / Judge / Warrior — Roger von Oech (*A Kick in the Seat of the Pants*, 1986; also *A Whack on the Side of the Head*, 1983)
- **Procedure:** run four separate passes: Explorer gathers odd material, Artist transforms
  it, Judge evaluates, Warrior commits and executes. Never let the Judge into the Artist's pass.

## L5 CONNECT
**Trap:** domain fixation and functional fixedness — searching only where you already know.

#### A Technique for Producing Ideas — James Webb Young (1940)
- **Move:** "an idea is nothing more or less than a new combination of old elements".
- **Procedure:** 1) Gather *specific* material (product, audience, brief) and *general*
  material (culture, adjacent fields) on separate cards. 2) Digest: pair cards two at a time
  and note odd partial combinations. 3) Drop the problem; work on something unrelated.
  4) Catch what surfaces. 5) Shape it against real constraints and criticism.
- **Use for:** campaigns, concepts, any brief rich in raw material. Works well as a master loop.

#### Bisociation — Arthur Koestler (*The Act of Creation*, 1964)
- **Move:** discovery happens where two unrelated frames of reference collide.
- **Procedure:** 1) Name the problem's frame (A). 2) Pick an unrelated, richly structured
  frame (B: a ritual, a sport, a craft, a genre). 3) Read the problem through B's rules.
  4) Keep the collision that makes both frames click.
- **Stories:** Archimedes (the crown problem × water rising in the bath). Koestler also tells
  Gutenberg's press as a collision of the wine-press and the seal — treat that as Koestler's
  narrative rather than documented history.

#### Far-analogy transfer — Dedre Gentner (structure-mapping, 1983); Franke, Poetz & Schreier (*Management Science*, 2014)
- **Procedure:** 1) Strip the problem to its relationships without nouns ("a fragile thing
  must survive impact", not "a phone case"). 2) List ≥5 domains facing the same relationship,
  from near to far. 3) Map structure one-to-one; ignore surface features. 4) Carry the solution
  across. 5) Make it usable — far analogies are more novel but less immediately usable.
- **Evidence:** solvers from analogous markets produced substantially more novel ideas, and
  the effect grew with distance — but other studies find a distance sweet spot; one far probe
  per round.
- **Story:** Rutherford's atom borrowed the solar system's *relationships* (orbiting around a
  centre), not its attributes (the sun's heat).

#### Synectics — William J.J. Gordon (1961) & George Prince, Arthur D. Little
- **Move:** "make the strange familiar and the familiar strange".
- **Procedure:** four analogies, run in turn: *personal* (become the object: "I am the
  seizing piston — what do I feel?"), *direct* (how does nature or another field solve it?),
  *symbolic* (compress the problem into a two-word oxymoron, a "book title": "dependable
  intermittency"), *fantasy* (solve it with magic, then work back to the possible).
- **Note:** popular success stories attached to Synectics (space-suit closures, stacked
  chips) are lore without sources; cite the method, not those stories.

#### Biomimicry — Eiji Nakatsu (JR West 500 Series Shinkansen, 1997)
- **Procedure:** 1) State the function needed. 2) Ask which organism already performs it
  under similar constraints. 3) Copy the principle, not the look.
- **Story:** the 500 Series made a booming noise exiting tunnels. Nakatsu, a birdwatcher,
  shaped the nose like a kingfisher's beak (which enters water with little splash) and used
  owl-feather serrations to quiet the pantograph. Claimed results: about 10% faster and about
  15% less electricity.

#### Cross-domain transfer — Claude Shannon (MIT master's thesis, 1937)
- **Story:** Shannon had met Boole's logic in an introductory philosophy course at Michigan;
  his thesis showed Boolean algebra could describe relay and switching circuits — a
  foundation of digital computing.
- **Procedure:** ask which formal system from another field already describes your problem.

#### Combinational synthesis — Johannes Gutenberg (c. 1440s)
- **Story:** movable metal type, an oil-based ink, and a press similar to agricultural screw
  presses, combined into one workflow. No single part was new; the combination was.
- **Procedure:** list mature components from different trades; look for the combination that
  creates a new system.

#### Exaptation & the adjacent possible — Gould & Vrba (*Paleobiology*, 1982); Stuart Kauffman; Steven Johnson (*Where Good Ideas Come From*, 2010)
- **Move:** a trait evolved for one purpose gets co-opted for another; new possibilities open
  only one step beyond what exists.
- **Procedure:** list the side effects and by-products of the existing thing; ask what each
  could be used for; ask what just became possible that wasn't last year.

#### Slip-box collisions — Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten (~90,000 cards; dozens of books and hundreds of articles)
- **Procedure:** link the current problem to notes from unrelated projects (the user's other
  projects, past concepts, the graveyard of earlier sessions) and look for collisions.

# STRUCTURAL family — force the space

## S1 MAP & DISRUPT
**Trap:** tunnel vision on familiar combinations; copying the category's conventions.

#### Morphological analysis (Zwicky box) — Fritz Zwicky
- **Move:** list every independent dimension and every value, then explore combinations you'd
  never reach by association. Zwicky applied it to jet and rocket propulsion at Aerojet in the
  1940s.
- **Procedure:** 1) Choose 4–6 independent parameters (for a music video: location, camera
  rule, performer, time structure, visual twist, ending). 2) List 4–8 values each. 3) Draw
  random paths (`draw.py` or a rule you didn't choose) and deliberately odd paths. 4) Develop
  the three most surprising coherent paths.

#### SCAMPER — Alex Osborn (*Applied Imagination*, 1953) → Bob Eberle (1971)
- **Procedure:** run the existing thing through Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify/Magnify,
  Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse. One idea per verb minimum.
- **Note:** weak evidence as a quality booster; useful as a fast variety generator.

#### Convention audit + ERRC grid — W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne (*Blue Ocean Strategy*, 2004–05); Jean-Marie Dru's "Disruption" (1990s)
- **Procedure:** 1) List the 6–12 factors the category competes on (Dru's "conventions").
  2) Draw the strategy canvas: how strongly the main competitors deliver each. 3) Eliminate a
  taken-for-granted factor. 4) Reduce one well below standard. 5) Raise one well above.
  6) Create one the category never offered. 7) Redraw: if your line runs parallel to the
  competitors', start again.
- **Story:** Cirque du Soleil dropped performing animals and star acts and built each show
  around a theme and storyline, growing while the circus industry declined.
- **Use for:** brands, show formats, artist positioning, product lines.

## S2 RESOLVE
**Trap:** accepting the trade-off ("stronger means heavier", "cheaper means worse").

#### TRIZ — Genrich Altshuller (from studying hundreds of thousands of patents; 40 principles, 1969)
- **Move:** the best inventions eliminate a contradiction instead of compromising on it.
- **Procedure:** 1) State the contradiction: "we want A to improve, but then B gets worse"
  (technical) or "it must be X and not-X" (physical). 2) For physical contradictions, separate
  in time, in space, by condition, or between part and whole. 3) Try the inventive principles
  most useful outside engineering: segmentation; taking out; local quality; asymmetry; merging;
  universality; nesting; prior action; do it in reverse; dynamics; periodic action; self-service;
  cheap short-lived objects; copying; "blessing in disguise" (turn harm into benefit).
- **Note:** TRIZ improves some metrics and worsens others in studies; use it to break the
  trade-off frame.

#### Evaporating Cloud — Eliyahu Goldratt (Thinking Processes, popularised in *It's Not Luck*, 1994)
- **Procedure:** 1) Write the common objective. 2) Write the two needs it requires. 3) Write
  the two conflicting wants each need seems to demand. 4) Surface the assumptions under each
  arrow. 5) Break one assumption — the conflict "evaporates".

#### The multi-problem test — Shigeru Miyamoto (as relayed by Satoru Iwata, *Ask Iwata*, 2021)
- **Move:** a good idea solves several problems at once.
- **Procedure:** 1) List every open problem (technical, budget, story, UX, brand). 2) Count how
  many each candidate solves. 3) Promote ideas solving three or more. 4) For one-problem ideas,
  ask what tweak would also solve another.
- **Story:** Mario's cap removed the need to animate hair; the moustache and big nose made the
  face readable without drawing a mouth; contrasting overalls made arm movement visible — one
  design answering the hardware's limits.

## S3 CONSTRAIN
**Trap:** the blank page and resource fixation — unlimited options produce mush.

#### Lateral thinking with withered technology — Gunpei Yokoi (Nintendo)
- **Move:** use cheap, mature, well-understood technology in a new way.
- **Procedure:** 1) List what is cheap, reliable and everywhere. 2) Ask what new use it enables.
- **Story:** the Game & Watch (1980) reused cheap calculator-style LCD technology (Yokoi
  supposedly got the idea watching a bored commuter fiddle with a calculator — anecdotal).
  The Game Boy's modest monochrome screen beat better-specced colour rivals on price and
  battery life.
- **Use for:** AI pipelines (use the reliable mode of a model in an unexpected way), low budgets.

#### Frugal improvisation (jugaad) — Mansukhbhai Prajapati, MittiCool
- **Story:** after losses in the Gujarat earthquake, the potter Prajapati built a clay
  refrigerator that cools by evaporation and needs no electricity. (Don't cite a number of
  days it keeps food; that figure isn't verified.)
- **Procedure:** solve with the materials and craft already in the room.

#### Constraint vows — Dogme 95 (von Trier & Vinterberg, 1995); *The Five Obstructions* (2003); Oulipo (1960); Stravinsky in *Poetics of Music*
- **Move:** binding rules remove the medium's crutches and force invention. Stravinsky argued
  that self-imposed constraints free the artist rather than limit him.
- **Procedure:** 1) Write 5–10 vows that remove the usual crutches (Dogme's included: location
  only, sound only from the scene, handheld camera, no special lighting, no genre films).
  2) Make the work. 3) Have a rival impose one new obstruction aimed at the creator's habits
  ("no shot longer than 12 frames", "remake it as a cartoon"). 4) Remake. 5) For words: Oulipo
  operations — lipogram (drop a letter), N+7 (replace each noun with the 7th after it in a
  dictionary), snowball (each line one letter longer).
- **Stories:** *Festen* (1998) was the first Dogme film. In *The Five Obstructions*, Jørgen
  Leth remade a film with no shot longer than 12 frames. Georges Perec wrote a novel without
  the letter "e".
- **Use for:** music videos, low-budget film, AI video that would otherwise look generic, lyrics.

# SYSTEMS family — see the whole

## Y1 LEVERAGE
**Trap:** fixing parts locally while the system's structure keeps producing the problem.

#### Leverage points — Donella Meadows ("Places to Intervene in a System", 1999)
- **Move:** numbers and parameters are the weakest levers; rules, information flows, goals
  and paradigms are the strongest. The highest is the power to transcend paradigms.
- **Procedure:** 1) List proposed interventions. 2) Classify each (parameter, buffer, feedback,
  information flow, rule, self-organisation, goal, paradigm). 3) Push each idea one level up
  the ladder ("change the rule, not the number").

#### Theory of Constraints — Eliyahu Goldratt (*The Goal*, 1984)
- **Procedure (five focusing steps):** 1) Identify the constraint. 2) Exploit it (get the most
  from it). 3) Subordinate everything else to it. 4) Elevate it (add capacity). 5) Repeat — the
  constraint moves. Improving anything that isn't the constraint is an illusion.

#### Standardise the box — Malcom McLean (container shipping)
- **Story:** McLean recalled waiting most of a day in 1937 to unload his truck at a port.
  On 26 April 1956 the Ideal-X sailed from Newark to Houston with 58 containers; early
  estimates put loading costs at about $5.86 a ton by hand versus roughly $0.16 by container.
- **Procedure:** find the universal interface that lets separate parts of a system connect
  without re-handling (a format, a template, a protocol, a standard unit).

#### Whole-system design — Amar Bose (901 speaker), Buckminster Fuller, Gordon Murray
- **Bose:** in 1956 he bought expensive speakers that measured well and sounded wrong; his
  research into how reflected sound reaches listeners in concert halls led to the 901 (1968),
  with eight of nine drivers facing the wall behind. The product was the room + ear, not the box.
- **Fuller:** synergetics and the geodesic dome — doing more with less through whole-structure
  geometry.
- **Murray:** the McLaren F1 (1992) was designed as one integrated system around low weight;
  his firm states that weight is the enemy of speed, handling, braking and feeling. The T.50
  revived the fan of his Brabham BT46B.
- **Procedure:** draw the whole experience end to end (including the environment and the
  person); optimise the interactions, not the parts.

#### OODA loop — John Boyd (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act)
- **Move:** cycle faster than the opponent and you shape their reality.
- **Procedure:** shorten every loop between making something and seeing the audience's
  reaction; ship small, observe, reorient.

## Y2 ROOT & FIRST PRINCIPLES
**Trap:** treating symptoms; reasoning by analogy ("it's always done this way").

#### Five Whys + genchi genbutsu — Taiichi Ohno (*Toyota Production System*, 1978), credited to Sakichi Toyoda's attitude
- **Procedure:** go and see the actual place; ask "why?" until you reach a cause you can fix
  for good. Ohno's own chain: a machine stopped → overloaded fuse → insufficient bearing
  lubrication → pump not drawing oil → worn pump shaft → no strainer, so metal scrap got in.
  Fix the strainer, not the fuse.
- **Note:** too basic alone for deep multi-cause problems — branch the whys.

#### Poka-yoke (mistake-proofing) — Shigeo Shingo (*Zero Quality Control*, 1986)
- **Move:** human error is certain; design the geometry so the error can't happen.
- **Procedure:** for each recurring error, design a physical or structural block (asymmetric
  connectors, a SIM card's notched corner, a form that won't submit with a bad value) instead of
  a reminder.

#### The 30-second demonstration — Richard Feynman (Rogers Commission, 11 February 1986)
- **Story:** at the televised Challenger hearing, Feynman put a clamped sample of O-ring
  material in ice water and showed it stayed deformed — cutting through pages of analysis.
- **Procedure:** strip the claim to the simplest physical or experiential demonstration;
  if you can't demonstrate it, you may not understand it.

#### Energy-maneuverability theory — John Boyd with Thomas Christie (1960s)
- **Story:** Boyd reduced air combat to how fast an aircraft can trade between energy states,
  overturning the heavier-and-faster doctrine; his "Fighter Mafia" shaped the F-15 and the
  Lightweight Fighter program that produced the F-16.
- **Procedure:** find the fundamental quantity that actually decides the outcome and design for it.

# HUMAN family — see the person

## H1 INHABIT
**Trap:** designing for an imagined average user — or for yourself.

#### Jobs to Be Done — Clayton Christensen (with Bob Moesta and colleagues)
- **Procedure:** 1) Find the struggling moment ("When I…, I want to…, so I can…"). 2) Ask what
  the person fires to hire this. 3) The real competition is whatever else does the job.
- **Story:** about 40% of a chain's milkshakes were bought first thing in the morning by
  commuters, to go. The job: make a long, boring drive bearable and stay full until lunch —
  competing with bagels, bananas and boredom, not other milkshakes.

#### Outcome-Driven Innovation — Tony Ulwick
- **Procedure:** break the job into steps and desired outcomes; score each outcome's importance
  and satisfaction; Opportunity = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0). Aim creativity
  at the highest-opportunity outcomes only.

#### Lead users — Eric von Hippel (1986)
- **Procedure:** find the extreme users who already hacked a solution (power users, fans,
  modders, pros); study the hack; productise the need behind it.

#### Prosthetic empathy — Patricia Moore (1979–1982)
- **Story:** at 26, the designer disguised herself as a woman in her eighties and travelled to
  116 cities in 14 US states and two Canadian provinces, experiencing products and spaces as an
  older person — a foundation of universal design (*Disguised*, 1985).
- **Procedure:** simulate the audience's real constraints (age, fatigue, a crying toddler, one
  hand on a steering wheel, a phone at 2% battery) before ideating.

#### Live in the future + schlep-blindness scan — Paul Graham (2012)
- **Procedure:** 1) Work at the leading edge as a heavy user. 2) List what's missing that you
  actually need. 3) Flag ideas you've been avoiding because they're tedious — few compete on
  them. 4) Prefer a few people who need it urgently over many who'd mildly like it.
- **Story:** developers knew online payments were painful for years; Stripe took on the
  schlep.

#### Scratch the itch — Chester Carlson
- **Story:** tired of copying patent documents by hand, Carlson made the first xerographic copy
  on 22 October 1938. More than 20 companies, including IBM, turned it down; Battelle took it
  on in 1944 and licensed it to Haloid in 1946, which became Xerox.
- **Procedure:** start from a tedium the creator personally suffers; persist past rejection.

## H2 PERCEIVE
**Trap:** engineering the object when the real problem is perception or behaviour.

#### Hedonic reframing — Rory Sutherland (TED, 2009)
- **Story:** about £6 billion was spent on new track to shave roughly 40 minutes off the
  London–Paris Eurostar journey. Sutherland suggested a fraction of that on free Wi-Fi — or
  supermodels serving free fine wine — would make passengers want the trip to last longer.
- **Procedure:** ask "what would make the experience feel better, rather than be faster or
  bigger?" Price the psychological fix against the engineering fix.

#### Choice architecture / nudge — the Schiphol urinal fly
- **Story:** Schiphol's cleaning manager Jos van Bedaf (remembering target dots from his army
  days) had a fly etched near urinal drains in the early 1990s; Aad Kieboom publicised it. The
  oft-quoted 80% spillage reduction is an unpublished claim. Thaler & Sunstein cited it in
  *Nudge* (2008) as a favourite example.
- **Procedure:** give people an irresistible, effortless target that makes the desired
  behaviour the natural one.

#### The elevator mirror — *parable* (told by MIT's Richard Larson, crediting Russell Ackoff)
- **Story:** complaints about slow lifts reportedly vanished once mirrors were installed beside
  the doors; the problem was boredom, not speed. No building, date or data is documented —
  present it as a parable.

#### MAYA — Raymond Loewy ("Most Advanced, Yet Acceptable")
- **Procedure:** score novelty and typicality 1–5 each; give very novel ideas one familiar
  anchor and very familiar ideas one advanced element; keep ideas ≥3 on both.
- **Use for:** pop songs, visual styles, logos, product design, pitches.

#### SUCCESs — Chip & Dan Heath (*Made to Stick*, 2007)
- **Procedure:** score 0–2 on Simple (one-sentence core), Unexpected, Concrete, Credible,
  Emotional (a specific person who cares), Story; rewrite the weakest.
- **Use for:** taglines, loglines, explainers, pitches.

## H3 MINE
**Trap:** a generic message any competitor could say.

#### Inherent drama — Leo Burnett
- **Procedure:** 1) Interrogate the product or project: how it's made, who makes it, what's odd
  about it. 2) List 20 true facts; circle those with tension or emotion. 3) Dramatise one fact,
  possibly as a character, instead of borrowing an outside premise. 4) Swap test: could a
  competitor run it? Then back to 1.
- **Stories:** archetypes rooted in the product itself: the Jolly Green Giant, Tony the Tiger,
  the Marlboro Man. Modern cousins: Burger King's Moldy Whopper (real food rots), Volvo
  Trucks' Epic Split (steering precision).

#### Deep Hidden Meaning — Nile Rodgers / Chic
- **Procedure:** 1) Write the surface subject (a party, a dance, a product). 2) Name a private,
  specific truth underneath it. 3) Find a phrase or image that works on both levels. 4) A casual
  listener gets the surface; an insider feels the second layer.
- **Story:** "Le Freak" began as the doorman's curse when Rodgers and Bernard Edwards were
  turned away from Studio 54 on New Year's Eve 1977; it became "Freak out".
- **Use for:** songs, artist concepts, titles, taglines.

#### The creator's own truth
- **Procedure:** ask the creator (or read their material for) the thing they know from the
  inside that outsiders don't: the scar, the habit, the place, the culture. Personal projects
  are strongest when the idea could only have come from this person.

# CRAFT family — domain-native form

## C1 SEQUENCE (story & image)
**Trap:** "and then" storytelling; telling instead of showing; pretty images with no idea.

#### But / Therefore — Trey Parker & Matt Stone (NYU class, 2011)
- **Procedure:** write the beats as one-liners; put "but" or "therefore" between each pair; any
  pair that only takes "and then" is a broken link — cut, merge or rewrite. Alternate
  consequence (therefore) and reversal (but) to escalate.

#### Story Spine — Kenn Adams (c. 1991; Pixar rule #4) · Story Circle — Dan Harmon
- **Spine:** Once upon a time… / Every day… / But one day… / Because of that… (×3) / Until
  finally… / And ever since then…
- **Circle:** comfort → want → unfamiliar situation → adapt → get it → pay a heavy price →
  return → changed.
- **Procedure:** write five spines with different "But one day…" lines; map the best onto the
  circle to check the character changes.

#### Kishōtenketsu — classical four-part structure; used in Nintendo level design (Koichi Hayashida)
- **Procedure:** Ki — introduce A. Shō — develop A without conflict. Ten — bring in an
  unrelated B (the twist). Ketsu — reconcile A and B so the pairing creates the meaning.
  In games: introduce a mechanic, develop it, twist it, then drop it.
- **Use for:** short-form video, kids' content, comedy, ads, four-panel comics, levels.

#### The ironic logline — Blake Snyder (*Save the Cat*, 2005)
- **Procedure:** the hero is the worst possible person for this situation; the logline gives a
  compelling mental picture, audience and cost, and a title that says what it is.

#### Suspense by information asymmetry; the Kuleshov effect; the MacGuffin — Alfred Hitchcock
- **Suspense vs surprise (Truffaut interviews, 1962; book 1966):** a bomb that explodes
  unexpectedly gives seconds of surprise; showing the audience the bomb first gives minutes of
  suspense.
- **Kuleshov effect:** Lev Kuleshov cut the same neutral face (Ivan Mozzhukhin) against soup,
  a girl in a coffin and a woman on a divan; audiences read different emotions. Hitchcock
  demonstrated it on his own face on CBC's *Telescope* (1964).
- **MacGuffin:** the thing the characters chase matters less than the chase.
- **Procedure:** decide who knows what and when; let context and cutting create the emotion.

#### Dialectical montage — Sergei Eisenstein
- **Move:** meaning comes from the collision of shots, not the shots themselves.
- **Procedure:** choose a method — metric (shot length), rhythmic (movement in frame), tonal
  (light, shape, texture), overtonal (all at once), intellectual (two images forcing a concept).
- **Story:** *Strike* (1925) intercuts the massacre of workers with the slaughter of cattle.
- **Use for:** music videos, trailers, edits, ad films.

#### Grid and reduction — Swiss / International Style (Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, 1981; Armin Hofmann; Bauhaus roots)
- **Procedure:** lock elements to a grid; use asymmetric balance; build hierarchy from scale,
  weight and space; remove anything decorative.
- **Use for:** posters, title design, brand systems, UI.

## C2 ENGINE (formats, comedy, songs, games)
**Trap:** hand-making one-off content instead of a system that keeps producing it.

#### Game of the Scene — Upright Citizens Brigade (*Comedy Improvisation Manual*, 2013; Del Close lineage)
- **Procedure:** establish a normal base reality; find the first unusual thing; ask "if this is
  true, what else is true?"; heighten the pattern three or more times at rising stakes; end on the
  biggest, cleanest version.
- **Use for:** sketches, sitcom engines, running gags, comedic ads, social series.

#### Track-and-hook, melodic math — the Cheiron / Max Martin approach (John Seabrook, *The Song Machine*, 2015)
- **Procedure:** start from the track; generate many short, condensed hook phrases (as syllables
  first); lyrics serve the melody — each line's syllables mirror its partner line; keep to a few
  contrasting melodic sections; write the words last.
- **Use for:** pop songs, jingles, kids' chants, prompts for music models.

#### Sid's rules — Sid Meier (written up by Soren Johnson, 2009)
- **Procedure:** every mechanic must create an interesting choice (a real trade-off); prototype
  to find the fun; change values by doubling or halving, not 5%; one good game beats two great
  ones — cut the competing system; the player should have the fun.

#### MDA — Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek (2004)
- **Procedure:** pick 1–3 target aesthetics (sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge,
  fellowship, discovery, expression, submission); design the dynamics that produce them; derive
  the mechanics. Playtest from the player's side.
- **Use for:** games, installations, participatory campaigns.

#### Formula as a feature — the series engine (e.g. *Phineas and Ferb*)
- **Procedure:** define the fixed beats every episode hits (the build, the parallel plot, the
  song, the near-miss) so that variation inside the formula becomes the joke. Test with ten
  episode loglines in ten minutes.

#### Make it a format, not a piece
- **Procedure:** ask what repeatable rule would let this idea run 100 times (weekly ritual,
  annual moment, template others can fill, challenge the audience recreates). Spotify Wrapped is
  a format; a single brand film is a piece.

# DIVERGENT family — volume, incubation, testing

## D1 DIVERGE & INCUBATE
**Trap:** premature convergence and perfectionism — loving the first workable idea.

#### Quota and cull — Dean Keith Simonton (equal-odds rule); 6-3-5 brainwriting (Bernd Rohrbach, 1968); Pixar rule #12
- **Procedure:** set a hard quota (30–100); generate in rounds (6-3-5: six voices, three ideas,
  six rounds = 108 ideas); discard the first, second and most obvious ideas by rule; judge only
  after the quota; keep a boneyard.

#### The invention factory — Thomas Edison (Menlo Park, 1876)
- **Story:** the first long-lasting lamp (October 1879) burned for about 13.5 hours on a
  carbonised thread; carbonised bamboo later lasted over 1,200 hours. The famous "10,000 ways that
  won't work" is a later embellishment: the earliest version (1910) is that he knew several
  thousand things that wouldn't work — said about his storage-battery experiments.
- **Procedure:** run many cheap, parallel experiments; log every failure as data.

#### High-frequency prototyping — James Dyson
- **Story:** 5,127 prototypes of the bagless cyclone vacuum between 1979 and 1984; his own-brand
  DC01 arrived in 1993.
- **Procedure:** make the cheapest testable version today; change one variable per iteration.

#### Dreamer / Realist / Critic — Robert Dilts' model of Walt Disney (*Strategies of Genius*, 1994)
- **Procedure:** three separated passes (or agents): the Dreamer with no limits; the Realist
  planning how it would actually be done; the Critic attacking the plan as questions. Loop the
  questions back to the Dreamer 2–3 times. Never mix the roles in one pass.

#### Incubation — Graham Wallas (*The Art of Thought*, 1926); Henri Poincaré
- **Procedure:** Preparation (load every constraint) → Incubation (step away) → Illumination
  (catch the flash) → Verification (test it ruthlessly).
- **Story:** Poincaré described his insight about Fuchsian functions arriving as he stepped onto
  an omnibus at Coutances, after he had stopped working on them.
- **Evidence:** a meta-analysis of 117 studies found a real but modest effect, larger for
  creative problems (Sio & Ormerod 2009). Linking incubation to the brain's default mode network
  is a hypothesis, not established. For an AI: a clean restart with an unrelated stimulus.

#### Immediate feedback — Seymour Papert (constructionism, *Mindstorms*, 1980); Bret Victor ("Inventing on Principle", 2012)
- **Move:** creators need an immediate connection to what they're making; ideas are found by
  building, not only by thinking.
- **Procedure:** prototype in the medium as early as possible (a rough animatic, a 10-second test
  generation, a clickable sketch) and react to it.

## D2 TEST
**Trap:** falling in love before checking — optimism bias.

#### Pre-mortem — Gary Klein (*HBR*, 2007)
- **Procedure:** "It's a year from now and this failed badly. Write the history of why." Then
  fix the plan today.
- **Evidence:** imagining the outcome as already having happened increased the number of
  reasons people generated by about 30% (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington 1989) — more reasons, not
  proven more accurate ones.

#### Working Backwards (PR/FAQ) — Amazon (Colin Bryar & Bill Carr, *Working Backwards*, 2021)
- **Procedure:** write the press release for the finished thing (headline, the customer's
  problem, the solution, a customer quote) plus the hardest FAQ; if the release is dull,
  change the idea before building. AWS was developed through more than a year of PR/FAQs.

#### What would have to be true? — Roger Martin with A.G. Lafley (*Playing to Win*, 2013)
- **Procedure:** for each of 3–5 genuinely different options, list what would have to be true for
  it to work (without debating whether it is); find the conditions you're least sure of; test
  those first, cheapest test first; then choose.

#### Braintrust notes — Pixar (Ed Catmull, *Creativity, Inc.*, 2014)
- **Procedure:** peers diagnose where the story bleeds ("at the midpoint the stakes become
  logistical") without prescribing fixes; the director keeps authority and decides.

#### Dialectical inquiry — Schweiger, Sandberg & Ragan (1986)
- **Procedure:** for the top plan, build the strongest *alternative plan* (not just a critique);
  debate the two; synthesise. Beat consensus in group studies.

## Corrections log (myths not to repeat)

- "Po" and the square-wheels provocation are **de Bono's**, not Roger von Oech's.
- Edison's "10,000 ways that won't work" is a **later embellishment**; the earliest version
  says "several thousand", about the storage battery.
- Wald's "armour where the holes aren't" scene is a **reconstruction**; the memos are real.
- The Schiphol fly's 80% figure is an **unpublished claim**; van Bedaf conceived it; *Nudge*
  cited it, it didn't inspire it.
- "Simplify, then add lightness" was **adopted** by Chapman from William Stout's credo.
- The elevator-mirror story is a **parable**.
- Zwicky's "576 combinations / classified patents" and Synectics' space-suit / stacked-chip
  stories have **no source**.
- The Kremer Prize was £5,000 in 1959 and £50,000 by the time it was won in 1977.
- Incubation = default mode network is a **hypothesis**.
- Gutenberg's wine-press eureka is **Koestler's narrative**.
- The pre-mortem's 30% is about the **number of reasons**, not accuracy.
- Luhmann's output: "dozens of books, hundreds of articles" (counts vary by source).
- MittiCool's storage duration and Bob Moesta's exact role in the milkshake study are
  unverified; don't state them as fact.
