01 / Synthetic task

Investigate before redesigning

Read the supplied conversation +

user: Our repair shop misses dates. Last week: intake median 10 minutes, work 45 minutes, approval wait 2 days; 8 of 10 late jobs waited for approval. Same staffing next month. What should we investigate and test before redesigning the whole shop?

Why this approach

Queue diagnosis and a bounded test. The user asked what to investigate, not for a new shop concept.

A worked response

Approval wait is worth investigating: its reported median is two days, versus 10 minutes for intake and 45 for work. Eight of ten late jobs waited for approval. That is 80% of late jobs, not an 80% risk of lateness and not proof of causation. We do not know how often on-time jobs also waited. Track ready-for-approval, approval and completion timestamps, job complexity, and late/on-time status for all jobs. Test one earlier approval check using the existing team, with workload and overtime monitored. Compare approval wait and missed dates with comparable jobs; stop if it displaces necessary work. Do not promise a time saving from these summaries.

  "late_jobs_waiting_fraction": 0.8,
  "late_jobs_total": 10,
  "on_time_approval_denominator_available": false
}

What remains unknown: Reported medians describe different stages; they do not establish the bottleneck or a causal effect. No shop pilot was run.

02 / Synthetic task

A recommendation that can change

Read the supplied conversation +

user: Should we automate a weekly process? Setup costs $4,000; manual work costs $200/week. Automation still needs exceptions handled, but we have no exception estimate. Compare the decision and explain what missing evidence could reverse it. No purchase or implementation is authorized.

Why this approach

Decision analysis with sensitivity. Generate alternatives only if they help the actual decision.

A worked response

The zero-overhead payback is 20 weeks: $4,000 / $200. That is a lower bound, not a launch recommendation. With $50/week of exceptions and maintenance it becomes 26.67 weeks; at $200/week there is no payback. For an illustrative 26-week horizon, total weekly overhead must stay below $46.15 to produce positive net savings. The user did not supply that horizon. Ask for the useful lifetime, then measure exception volume and handling time before committing. No purchase or implementation is authorized.

03 / Synthetic task

A promising result is not rollout evidence

Read the supplied conversation +

user: Our original pilot rule was at least 20% lower median wait without more errors.

assistant: Please return the baseline, pilot results and relevant changes before interpretation.

user: Baseline: 40 jobs, median 50 minutes, 2 errors. Pilot: 5 jobs, median 35 minutes, 1 error. Pilot week was quieter and used our most experienced worker. Is it proven? Should we roll it out?

Why this approach

Evaluate against the original acceptance rule and check comparability before deciding readiness.

A worked response

Median wait fell from 50 to 35 minutes, a 30% reduction. The observed wait statistic clears the 20% target. The baseline has 2 errors in 40 jobs (5%); the pilot has 1 in 5 (20%). Raw error counts fell, but the error rate rose; clarify whether the original safety criterion meant counts or rates before deciding it passed. Five jobs, a quieter week and the most experienced worker prevent attribution to the new workflow. Do not call it proven or authorize rollout. Repeat a bounded comparison with normal staffing and comparable load, record both error counts and denominators, and retain the original wait target. An exploratory repeat can inform the decision; it cannot silently replace the agreed acceptance rule.

Inspect the calculated facts +

  "median_wait_reduction": 0.3,

  "baseline_jobs": 40,
  "pilot_jobs": 5,
  "observed_wait_target_met": true
}

What remains unknown: These are sample statistics, not a causal estimate or proof that the error rate truly increased. No new pilot was executed.
